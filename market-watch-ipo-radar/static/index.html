Where this comes from. Index and ETF prices come from Yahoo Finance via the yfinance library. Fed content comes from the Federal Reserve's own press-release RSS feeds, with the FOMC statement text extracted from the release page on federalreserve.gov.

What to watch out for. Yahoo's data is delayed, not live, and outside market hours the “last price” is simply the most recent close — so a 0.00% change may just mean the market is shut. Total US, total world and emerging-market rows track the VTI, VT and VWO ETFs, because Yahoo doesn't reliably carry the underlying indices; ETFs follow their index closely but are not identical to it and carry their own fees and tracking error. The FOMC summary is an automated extract of the first paragraphs of the press release, so it can miss context or vote details — always read the linked original.

This calendar is shorter than Yahoo's or Nasdaq's — it only lists deals with a confirmed date. Expect IPOs to be missing.

Where this comes from. Finnhub's IPO calendar endpoint, on a free API tier.

Why deals are missing. This feed lists deals that already have a confirmed expected pricing date — typically companies a week or two from listing. Companies that have filed an S-1 with the SEC but haven't set a date yet do not appear here, and that pipeline is far larger than the confirmed list. Sites like Yahoo Finance and Nasdaq show those filings too, which is why their calendars look much fuller. Smaller, foreign and over-the-counter listings may also be absent. We have not verified how this free tier's coverage compares to Finnhub's paid tiers, so assume this is a floor, not a complete picture.

Details change. Offer prices shown as a range are the filing range, not the final price. Dates slip, price ranges get revised, and deals get pulled outright — sometimes hours before listing.

Not financial advice. Before drawing any conclusion about a deal here, go to the source: the company's S-1/424B prospectus on SEC EDGAR and the underwriters' final terms. Note also that the offer price shown is the price institutions pay at allocation, not a price generally available on the open market.

Where this comes from. Deal facts — ticker, company, date, offer price, shares offered, exchange — come from Finnhub. Sector, industry, current price and all performance figures come from Yahoo Finance, looked up per ticker.

Est. market cap at IPO is genuinely an estimate. It's the offer price multiplied by the company's current shares outstanding, because no free source publishes at-IPO share counts. Any company that has issued or bought back stock since listing will be overstated or understated — sometimes badly. Treat it as an order of magnitude, not a figure to quote.

Gaps and mislabels. Coverage starts in late October 2021, so anything older is absent. Tickers that were acquired, delisted or renamed often return no market data and show as —. The SPAC tag is a heuristic based on company name and a $10.00 offer price, so it will occasionally mislabel an operating company, or miss a SPAC with an unusual name.

Not financial advice. Past IPO performance says nothing about future deals. Verify any specific company against SEC EDGAR and a primary market-data source before drawing conclusions about it.

How have recent IPOs performed?

Computed over the most recent priced IPOs in the lookback window. Each ticker needs its own Yahoo Finance lookup, so the first run takes a moment — results are cached afterwards.

Choose your options and hit “Run analysis”.
These averages are flattered by survivorship bias — roughly a fifth of the IPOs have vanished from the data. Read them as directional, not as a measured return.

How these numbers are built. General metrics cover the entire dataset rather than a sample of it — all 869 non-SPAC deals Finnhub lists for the window. That is not the same as every IPO that took place: this feed misses deals, as the calendar tab explains, so read it as a census of what we can see, not of the market. That takes several hundred price lookups, so the finished snapshot is cached and recomputed periodically rather than on every load; Recalculate forces a fresh one. The breakdown further down is different: it samples the most recent N deals, and that sample size is yours to change.

Prices are split-adjusted back to what actually traded. Yahoo restates old prices for later stock splits, while the offer price is the original unadjusted figure. Compared naively, a failed micro-cap that did repeated reverse splits shows up as a six-figure percentage gain — one in this dataset computed to +100,210,425% before the correction. Every historical close here is multiplied back by the splits since listing.

Survivorship bias is the big one. Companies that went bankrupt, got delisted or were acquired often return no price data, so they silently drop out of the averages. You can see the size of it: of the 869 IPOs in our dataset for this window, only about 674 still return a current price. The missing ~19% are overwhelmingly failures rather than successes, so every return figure here is more optimistic than reality.

Other things that skew it. The sample is the most recent IPOs, so it's weighted toward companies that have had little time to fall — recency bias on top of survivorship. Sectors with only one or two IPOs produce averages that are noise, so check the count in the tooltip before believing a bar. “First month” means roughly 21 trading days measured from the offer price, which is not a return most investors could have earned — retail buyers typically enter at the day-one open, often well above offer. SPACs are excluded by default via a name-and-price heuristic that isn't perfect.

Not financial advice. This is exploratory analysis on free data, not research. It's a guide to where to look next — do proper due diligence from primary sources before drawing any investment conclusion.
